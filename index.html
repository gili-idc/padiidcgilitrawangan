PADI IDC Gili Trawangan: The Complete Guide to Becoming a Dive Instructor

Thinking about turning your love of the ocean into a career? This guide explains how the PADI Instructor Development Course works on Gili Trawangan, what you will learn, who it suits and how to prepare.

Every dive professional remembers the moment they decided to teach. Maybe it was a nervous student who finally relaxed on their first breath underwater, or a day when you realised that sharing the sea felt better than simply exploring it. The step from certified divemaster to PADI instructor is the one that makes that feeling a profession, and for many people the PADI IDC on Gili Trawangan is where that step begins.

The Gili Islands sit just off the northwest coast of Lombok, in Indonesia. They are known for warm water, gentle conditions, turtles on almost every dive and a relaxed island rhythm with no cars on the roads. For a course that asks you to think, teach and be assessed every day, that setting matters. You can concentrate, recover and enjoy the evenings with other candidates who are working toward the same goal.

In this guide we cover everything a candidate usually asks before booking: what the IDC actually is, how the training is organised, what the Instructor Examination involves, what happens afterwards and how to judge whether a training centre is right for you.

What Is the PADI IDC?

IDC stands for Instructor Development Course. It is the professional programme, run by a PADI Course Director, that prepares certified divemasters to teach scuba diving to the PADI standard. The course does not simply check that you can dive well. It teaches you how to plan lessons, manage students, solve problems in the water and present information clearly to people with very different learning styles.

You can think of the IDC as the bridge between being a good diver and being a good teacher. A strong diver can usually perform every skill. A strong instructor can explain why the skill matters, demonstrate it in slow motion, notice who in the group is struggling and adjust the lesson without losing the rest of the class. Most of the IDC is built around developing those habits.

The IDC is followed by the PADI Instructor Examination, usually shortened to the IE. The IE is a two-day assessment run by a PADI examiner. Once you pass, you become a PADI Open Water Scuba Instructor and can teach a wide range of PADI courses worldwide.

Why Choose Gili Trawangan for Your Instructor Course?

Location is more than scenery. A good IDC location offers calm water for confined water practice, easy access to open water sites, reliable logistics and a community where learning is the main activity. Gili Trawangan ticks these boxes. The island is small enough to walk around, so travelling between the classroom, the pool, the dive boats and your accommodation takes minutes rather than hours.

The reefs around the Gili Islands also make excellent teaching sites. Visibility is often good, the sea is warm for most of the year, and the variety of dives lets candidates practise skills in different conditions, from shallow slopes to light current. Green and hawksbill turtles are regular visitors, which keeps the dives enjoyable even when you are concentrating on technique.

There is also the human side. Because candidates arrive from many countries, the evenings turn into informal study groups. People swap lesson ideas, practise explanations over dinner and cheer each other on before exam days. Many graduates say the friendships formed on the island were as valuable as the certificate itself.

How the PADI IDC Is Structured

The IDC is made up of several connected parts. Although the exact schedule differs between centres, the core elements are consistent because they follow PADI standards.

Knowledge development

You study teaching theory, PADI standards and procedures, dive theory, risk management and the instructor's role in the dive community. Classroom sessions are interactive. You present short lessons to your fellow candidates, receive feedback and then try again, which is the fastest way to improve.

Confined water teaching

In a pool or a shallow, calm area, you practise presenting skills the way you will teach them to students. You learn to demonstrate each skill clearly, to break it into steps, to correct common mistakes and to keep every student safe and comfortable.

Open water teaching

Here you apply the same approach in the sea, where conditions are less predictable. You practise briefing, supervising, skill demonstration and debriefing, all while managing the environment around you.

Emergency First Response Instructor training

Most programmes include the Emergency First Response Instructor course, which qualifies you to teach CPR and first aid. This adds real value to your instructor profile, because many students want a rescue or first aid course alongside their diving.

Learning to Teach, Not Just to Dive

A common surprise for new candidates is how much of the IDC is about communication. You will be asked to explain one idea in two minutes, then to explain it again in a completely different way for a student who learns visually. You will be asked to handle a scenario where a student panics, a mask floods or equipment is missing, and to keep the class calm while you solve the problem.

Rescue skills receive special attention. Instructors are responsible for the safety of everyone in the water, so you will practise assisting a tired or unresponsive diver, managing the surface and giving emergency care. Candidates often describe these sessions as the most demanding part of the week, and also the most rewarding, because the skills are directly useful in real life.

Good training centres give detailed feedback after each presentation. Instead of a general comment such as "nice job", you hear exactly what worked, which sentence could be shorter and where you could stand to improve the students' view. That precise feedback is what turns nervous candidates into confident teachers.

Who Is the PADI IDC For?

The course suits several kinds of candidates. Newly certified divemasters often continue straight into the IDC while their knowledge is fresh. Experienced divers who have been guiding for years may want the formal teaching qualification that opens up better jobs. Divers from other agencies sometimes choose a crossover route, which we discuss in a later guide in this series.

PADI sets minimum requirements for the IDC and the Instructor Examination. In general, you need to be at least 18 years old, hold a PADI Divemaster or an equivalent professional certification, have current Emergency First Response training, provide a medical statement signed by a physician and have a minimum number of logged dives before the examination. Because standards can be updated, always confirm the current list with your training centre before you travel.

Beyond paperwork, the best candidates share a few traits. They are curious about how people learn, patient with beginners and willing to accept feedback. You do not need to be a natural speaker. Many outstanding instructors started out shy, and learned to teach by practising in a supportive environment.

Quick checklist: certified divemaster or equivalent, 18 years or older, current EFR training, medical statement, logged dives, and a genuine wish to teach.

The PADI Instructor Examination

The IE is the final step toward becoming a PADI instructor. It is a two-day evaluation conducted by a PADI examiner who is independent from your training team. You complete knowledge assessments, and you present lessons in both confined and open water, showing that you can teach to the PADI standard.

Many candidates feel nervous about the IE, which is natural. The best way to handle that nervousness is thorough preparation. A good IDC includes mock presentations that follow the same format as the real exam, so by the time you enter the examination you already know what to expect. You will have practised each lesson several times and received feedback on how to improve.

It is also worth remembering that the examiner wants you to succeed. The exam is a measurement of whether you meet the standard, and it is passed by candidates who prepare, stay calm and teach clearly. If something goes wrong during a presentation, recovering gracefully is a skill in itself.

Life and Career After Certification

Once you hold your PADI Open Water Scuba Instructor certificate, a wide range of paths opens up. You can teach beginner courses at a dive resort, work on a liveaboard, lead guided dives for certified customers or move into specialty teaching. Many instructors begin in popular dive destinations across Southeast Asia, where demand for English-speaking and multilingual teachers is strong.

Others combine diving with travel, working seasonally in different countries. Some build skills toward higher PADI levels, including Master Scuba Diver Trainer and Specialty Instructor ratings, which expand the courses you can offer. A few eventually become course directors themselves.

Whatever route you choose, the habits learned during the IDC stay with you: clear communication, careful planning, calm leadership and a genuine interest in helping other people enjoy the underwater world.

What a Typical IDC Week Looks Like

Days on the IDC are full but varied. A typical morning might begin with a classroom presentation in which each candidate teaches a short theory lesson to the group. The Course Director then gives feedback, and the group repeats the exercise with improvements. After that, the schedule often moves to the pool or the sea, where you practise skill demonstrations and learn how to organise a group of students around a single exercise.

Afternoons usually combine further teaching practice with self-study. Many candidates spend the evenings reviewing the manuals, planning their next presentation and helping each other rehearse. The workload is real, but it is balanced by the island setting. A short walk to the beach after class, a quiet sunset and a shared meal help candidates reset for the next day.

As the course moves forward, you will notice that your confidence grows quickly. Early presentations often feel stiff, with too much reading from notes. By the second week most candidates speak more naturally, use the whiteboard well and respond calmly to unexpected questions from their classmates.

How to Prepare Before You Arrive

The more you prepare, the more you benefit from every session. Begin by reading through your Divemaster materials again, especially the sections on dive theory, physics and physiology. Refreshing this knowledge before the course saves time and lets you focus on teaching technique rather than relearning the basics.

Next, check that your paperwork is in order. Confirm that your Emergency First Response training is current, that your medical statement is ready and that your logbook is up to date. Bring copies of your certification cards and confirm the entry requirements with the training centre well in advance of your travel date.

Finally, look after your fitness and your rest. Instructor training involves long days, plenty of water time and a lot of concentration. Arriving rested, hydrated and curious will help you absorb feedback and enjoy the experience instead of simply surviving it.

How to Choose the Right IDC Centre

With many centres offering instructor training across Indonesia, it helps to compare them on the things that really affect your results. Look at the Course Director's experience, the size of the group, the number of presentation sessions, the amount of Instructor Examination support and the clarity of the total cost. Ask what is included, what is extra and how many hours you will spend with the Course Director.

Read reviews from former candidates, and pay attention to what they say about the teaching style rather than only the location. Recent comments that mention helpful feedback, supportive instructors and good exam preparation are a reliable sign of quality training.

Finally, trust your communication with the centre. If the team replies quickly, answers your questions honestly and gives you realistic expectations, that attitude will probably continue throughout the course.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to be a Divemaster before the PADI IDC?

Generally yes. You need a PADI Divemaster or an equivalent professional-level certification. If you are not there yet, a Divemaster programme is the first step.

Is the IDC the same as the Instructor Examination?

No. The IDC is the training course, and the IE is the final assessment run by a PADI examiner after the IDC.

Do I need to be an experienced teacher?

No. The course is designed to teach you how to teach. Willingness to practise and accept feedback matters more than previous experience.

Why train on Gili Trawangan?

The island offers warm water, convenient dive sites, small-group training and an international community of candidates, all within a short walk.

Where can I check dates and prices?

Dates and costs change, so check the current schedule on the official website or message the team directly for the latest information.

What should I bring to the course?

Bring your Divemaster materials, certification cards, logbook, medical statement and your preferred dive gear. Notebooks, a waterproof slate and a positive attitude are also useful, because you will be taking notes and planning lessons every single day.

Will I feel ready to teach after the IDC?

Most candidates do. The course is built around repeated practice and honest feedback, so by the Instructor Examination you will have taught many sessions and know how to handle common situations. Confidence continues to grow with your first real students, and good centres remain available for advice afterwards.
